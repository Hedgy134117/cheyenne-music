Great albums: January 2026

  1. My Ghosts go Ghost, by By Storm - I received this album with a lot of excitement, after being mind-boggled by Injury Reserve’s final project in 2022. Their first project since reforming as “By Storm” is a triumph through and through, showing the group experimenting with gentler, folktronica-influenced sounds. From the first track (“Can I Have You For Myself?”) showcases some of the most beautiful and acoustic sounds they’ve ever created, before exploding into a glitchy, disorienting waltz. This contrast between pastoral beauty and cacophony brings to mind artists like Xiu Xiu - a comparison that also stands when looking at instrumentation like the eerie, distorted violin on “Best Interest.” Coupled with the group’s lyrics about grief and personal strife, My Ghosts go Ghost can be a demanding album to unpack (even if it’s less apocalyptic than Injury Reserve’s prior work), but it’s undeniably poignant and interesting.

  2. 656, by Roc Marciano - drumless self-produced hip hop with an eerie, analog vibe. It feels both minimal and incredibly lush and well thought-out, especially when complemented by Roc’s head-scratching, cinematic lyricism. I actually am not sure if I’ve enjoyed a Roc Marciano album quite this much before this point, but he creates an incomparable jazzy/noir aura on “656” that works especially well, between his creaky samples and his knotty, complex verses.

  3. A-Rhythm Absolute, by Sunday Mourners - this album just scratched a really obvious itch for me, as someone who loves the punk and post-punk of the 70s and 80s. Vocally, this band reminds me of many greats in rock music - from Lou Reed to Julian Casablancas. They also prove themselves to be capable of a wide range of rock subgenres - there’s the kraut-y 12 minute “Darling,” as well as the angular post-punk of opener “Careers In Acting.”

  4. 波地獄 — Post Purgatory, by 破地獄/Scattered Purgatory - an awe-inspiring Taiwanese album by an incredibly experimental duo that I hadn’t heard of before this point. On “Post Purgatory,” which marks the end of a hiatus the band took during/after the pandemic, the band combine molasses-slow rushes of bass and guitar (very doom-y) with heavy-hitting, trip hop-influenced beats. There are also torrents of saxophone here and there that give the project a distinctly jazzy feel.

  5. e.t.d.s. MIXTAPE, by IDK - a really reinvigorating mixtape with lots of energy and an old school rap sound. The project largely concerns IDK’s feelings about a 15-year sentence he caught at age 17. He only did 3 years, but the time that he spent locked up haunts these tracks. That being said, he approaches his songwriting in a playful way that prevents the record from being particularly vulnerable. Songs like the Joey Valence & Brae-featured “CLOVER” bring in some obligatory sex bars, and the DMX-featured “START TO FiNISH” is just one example of the braggadocio IDK commits to on this project.

  6. Secret Love, by Dry Cleaning - while playing at the dry wit that gave them their breakout, Dry Cleaning move into a more expansive, uninhibited sound with production assistance from Cate Le Bon. In particular, opener “Hit My Head All Day” sees them move into groovy, 80’s post-disco territory.

  7. Stand On My Shoulders, by Ya Tseen - a dreamlike, deeply political record. The overall soundscape on this one is very slippery, with components slipping in and out of the mix, and Galanin’s approach is excitingly collaborative, with contributions ranging from Pink Siifu to Portugal the Man.

  8. As of Now, by Lord Jah-Monte Ogbon - a great record from the Charlotte rapper that shows his humor as well as his great flows. It’s also hard to take your eyes off of, as there is something interesting about every single track.

  9. Jana Horn, self titled - a sparse, but well-orchestrated singer-songwriter album that quietly explores the feeling of being lost, or searching for something. The quiet songs often have subtly elegant instrumental developments as they progress.

  10. I Used To Go To This Bar, by Joyce Manor - at only 19 minutes long, Joyce Manor offer a breezy, catchy pop punk record with great choruses.

Great albums: February 2026

  1. The Mountain, by Gorillaz - I’m still digesting this one since it’s only been out for around a week as of writing this, but I know for a fact that it is very good! On The Mountain, Damon Albarn steers his omnivorous musical interests in the direction of Indian classical music, and enlists talented musicians that include Anoushka Shankar, Asha Bhosle, and Asha Puthli (whose Indian pop/disco albums I highly recommend) to realize this sound for the album. Conceptually, the album focuses mostly on death, and even features posthumous contributions from previous Gorillaz collaborators like Dennis Hopper and Dave of De La Soul. Despite loving the singles “The Happy Dictator” and “Damascus,” I don’t think anything could have prepared me for how much I love this album - it is without a doubt their most well-executed creative vision since Plastic Beach.
  2. URGH, by Mandy, Indiana - on their sophomore project, the French electro-industrial duo really step it up, with beats that feel meaner and even more bass-boosted than their debut, and songs that feel significantly more fleshed out. The song structures are still very experimental (especially considering moments like the unexpected string outro in the glitched-out opener “Sevastopol”), but somehow pack in some unfathomable grooves - especially on lead single “Magazine.” Vocally, the band seem to be most influenced by hip hop, and bring in highly visceral energy as well as off-kilter, rhythmically surprising flows. From start to finish, URGH embodies chaos in an inventive and satisfying way.
  3. To Whom This May Concern, by Jill Scott - on her first album in 10 years, Jill Scott’s voice is both hopeful and lived-in. The warm instrumentals and production of this album work to center her vocals - particularly on tracks like “Offdaback” where she sings more conversationally over pensive piano chords. There are lots of flourishes over the record’s warm neo-soul palette, like the grandiose horns on “Be Great,” but none of them ever crowd Scott’s vocals and lyrics. Touching on themes of black joy, community and love, as well as reflection and aging (see the excellent jazzy single “Pressha”), To Whom This May Concern is a career-defining album and an early highlight of 2026.
  4. Laughter In Summer, by Beverly Glenn-Copeland - one of the most moving projects I’ve listened to in a long time. In 2023, music veteran Beverly Glenn-Copeland was diagnosed with dementia, a diagnosis that has left many desolate. After receiving this diagnosis, Glenn-Copeland decided to continue with his career, go on tour, and continue penning songs and reimagining older ones alongside his partner, Elizabeth Glenn-Copeland. The resulting album is a nourishing fusion of folk, new age, and neo-classical music, crafted with a community of Canadian musicians such as Helena Deland who were eager to uplift Glenn-Copeland and his artistry.
  5. The Apple Tree Under the Sea, by Hemlocke Springs - this was a long-awaited project for me! I have had my sights set on Hemlocke Springs since she debuted in 2023 with some of the best pop songs of the decade so far (particularly “girlfriend”), and her debut full-length record is every bit as playful, adventurous, and catchy as I hoped! But in the years she took to develop this project, she also honed in on some overarching concepts like temptation and unrequited love that make her an even more exciting voice in the pop landscape.
  6. Nothing’s About to Happen to Me, by Mitski - between this record and The Land is Inhospitable, I am realizing that Mitski’s voice and lyrics are such a natural fit for Americana that it’s incredible she didn’t experiment with it earlier. Lyrically, this new record is one of Mitski’s most ambitious as she puts herself in the shoes of a reclusive woman with an unkempt home. Within this character, she elegantly explores a host of themes like desperation (“I’ll Change for You”) and suicidal ideation (“Instead of Here”).
  7. The Pleasure is Yours, by Gena (Liv.e & Karriem Riggins) - a cosmic fusion between Riggins’ seasoned and extensive beatmaking and Liv.e’s fluid, unpredictable neo-soul. From the retro, neon-colored “This Is So Crazy” to the smoky “Lead It Up,” the duo achieve a lot of variety within their luxurious sound.
  8. MAN BITES DOG, by femtanyl - I put this on during my bus commute to work and was instantly swept into auditory euphoria like none other at 7 a.m. It’s a project so bitcrushed it’s like someone’s ramming a flail into both of your ears, and it completely rips.
  9. Marathon, by Maria BC - an uncannily beautiful gothic folk release that feels quietly aggressive in its textural depth, despite having a soft-spoken surface.
  10. Heaven Ain’t Sold, by Choker - after a 7 year hiatus, Choker has delivered a soft yet surprising record that warrants Frank Ocean comparisons more than anything else I’ve heard in years.

Great Albums: March 2026

  1. Sentence Structure in the Country, by more eaze - Spellbinding from start to finish. Historically, more eaze has not been the easiest artist for me to digest, but “Sentence Structure in the Country” clicked for me immediately. The field recordings, ambient hisses, and instrumentation work to create the most interesting soundscapes I’ve heard in a while - especially when more eaze adds autotuned, tinny vocals to the mix. The sonic palette of the album is unnerving, but brightly colored (especially on tracks like Distance that have more of an acoustic makeup) - almost sounding like an inverse of sounds created by fellow ambient boundary-pusher aya, who also has been releasing music that I love. Definitely a treat, and endlessly replayable in terms of wanting to unpeel the layers!
  2. How to Make a Breakfast, by Land Whales - an unkempt, distorted rock record hailing from Cuba that mixes and regurgitates everything from Franz Kafka’s “The Trial” to Sonic Youth’s “Daydream Nation.” In the meantime, they balance chaotic passages with sugary chorus melodies, showing a particular talent for weaving their way through a variety of 90’s-indebted alt-rock styles while making something completely unique.
  3. In Another, by Masahiro Takahashi - a sunkissed ambient jazz album that feels perfect as the weather thaws. Its uniqueness lies in how Takahashi indulges in chamber music and surreal electronic touches, with “Queen West Butterfly” being a particular standout - if you don’t check out anything else from this list, that track is a must-listen!
  4. Trying Times, by James Blake - at this point in his career, it feels like a guarantee that anything James Blake puts out is likely worth a listen. However, “Trying Times” delivered even more heft than I was expecting. It feels like a very genuine and direct effort from James, particularly since his vocals are made more front-and-center here than they were in the past. This often means that he leans into more traditional pop structures, but his music still sounds as shimmery and alien as ever. I really enjoyed the musical crescendo in “I Had a Dream She Took My Hand.”
  5. Honora, by Flea - As someone who could not give less of a shit about the Red Hot Chili Peppers’ music, I didn’t really have expectations when I heard that the bassist of Flea was making a jazz record. However, I am so glad that I ended up giving this a chance, because it is a wonderful effort. On it, Flea enlists musicians from the L.A. jazz scene to help elevate the album’s sound, and there are even vocal features from Thom Yorke and Nick Cave (who I love very much).
  6. A Rush to Nowhere, by Arima Ederra - on this record, Ederra presents a chameleonic take on R&B that puts her right in line with other innovators of the genre, like Mk.gee and Dijon. With feather-light vocals and arrangements, she concocts a sound that feels absolutely heavenly - particularly on moments like the chorus of “Gemini Eyes.”
  7. Hard Hearted Woman, by Ora Cogan - a project that excitedly pushes country and Americana music into gorgeous, ghostly directions. Its fusion of Americana’s rustic sound with gothic and psychedelic touches make it both a nostalgic and refreshing listen.
  8. Hurts Like Hell, by Charlotte Cornfield - a folk/Americana record whose strength lies in the detailed portraits Charlotte creates of her characters as they experience life and love.
  9. Girlfriend, by Grace Ives - an album that strikes a rare balance between intimate bedroom pop and shimmering, nocturnal synthpop.
  10. Hinterlands, by Green-House - yet another serene, nature-inspired release from these ambient auteurs.
  11. Dying Is The Internet, by Simo Cell & Abdullah Miniawy - a daring combination of bombastic, thundering electronics and vocals inspired by Arabic poetry.
  12. Paradises, by Ladytron - amidst blissed-out synths, “Paradises” sees the electroclash group indulge more pop-centric impulses.
  13. I Guess U Had to Be There, by E L U C I D & Sebb Bash - a refreshingly direct hip-hop effort from this rapper-producer duo that navigates some truly eerie, minimalist beats.
  14. Future Present Past, by Irreversible Entanglements - a very complex effort led by vocalist Moor Mother that draws from across the black diaspora to make a refreshing, hopeful record.
  15. Companion, by Sluice - a straightforward folk rock release brought to life by earnest songwriting and interesting textures.

BONUS: Just a Few Excellent EPs - March 2026

  1. BabyGoated, by Amy Gadiaga - there’s uplifting neo-soul, inspiring jazz, and experimental impulses in spades on this new Amy Gadiaga EP. “BabyGoated” cements her as not only a fantastic performer and arranger, but one of the most exciting recent voices in the London jazz scene.
  2. Is This All There Is? By Anna Calvi - there isn’t much I could write that would sell it more than the tracklist itself: there’s a propulsive, Iggy Pop-featured gothic banger (which is one of the best new songs I’ve heard of late), as well as covers of Bonnie “Prince” Billy and Kraftwerk that feature Perfume Genius and Laurie Anderson, respectively. Check it out!!!
  3. Swan Songs, by Model/Actriz - another Model/Actriz release, another opportunity for me to meatride them into the sunset on this website. Please check this out for more industrial-tinged ragers!
  4. The Weight, by Cashier - 6 propulsive, lo-fi pop punk numbers that cover the classic coming-of-age/lovelorn territory of emo and pop punk.
  5. I Sure Am Getting Sick of This Bowling Alley, by Mclusky - a brief, but solid effort that proves that Mclusky are still fantastic noise-rockers.
  6. It’s Glass, by Dutch Interior - after a particularly great studio album last year, Dutch Interior are already back with more downtempo, Americana-brushed folk tracks.
  7. (Un)planted, by midori jaeger - ethereal, cello-driven indie pop that finds flowy propulsion in naturalistic textures.
  8. BIG MAMA, by Flying Lotus - an unpredictable, wildly fun EP that sees Flying Lotus do a full 180 into hyperactive, cartoonish electronic music.
  9. Temesgen, by ZENA - a London based Ethio-jazz effort that combines the genre’s tonality with global influences from Latin and soul music.
  10. 97, by Ana Tijoux & DJ Dacel - a Spanish-language conscious hip hop record that leans into a jazzy, old school sound.

Great Albums: April 2026

  1. MR COBRA, by Lucy Liyou - for a very, very patient listener, this album is the most rewarding listening experience of April, fullstop. Equal parts performance art, musique concrete, free jazz, sound collage, and opera, “MR COBRA” interrogates the humiliation of revisiting trauma - in this case, Lucy’s falling in love with a predator when she was in high school. Desire, shame, and victimhood intersect with Lucy’s transness to unsettling effect. All the while, she works in references from Taylor Swift to “Rosemary’s Baby” to share (and seemingly, relive) her story.
  2. Superbloom, by Jessie Ware - After starting the 2020’s with 2 highly acclaimed nu-disco albums, “Superbloom” sees Jessie elaborate on this nostalgic sound with a little more kitsch, and highly grandiose orchestration. Rather than disco, “Superbloom” harkens to 70’s soul/R&B divas, pre-”Disco Demolition”, and reaches glorious heights on the title track and “Automatic” (one of my personal favorite songs of the year so far). Elsewhere “Mr Valentine” offers a more spare, bass-heavy side of disco that calls back to ESG.
  3. My New Band Believe, self titled - a richly detailed and theatrical solo debut from former Black Midi bassist Cameron Picton. The theatrics and chamber arrangements remind me quite a bit of BCNR’s recent output, but where BCNR tended to take their instrumentation in a 2000’s indie twee direction, “My New Band Believe” feels like pure medieval whimsy. The quick tempos on this also give it the urgency of Black Midi’s music, but the spaciousness of the arrangements give the record a much less claustrophobic sound. I enjoy it a lot - especially “Love Story,” which moves from domestic bliss to cacophonous tragedy.
  4. Our Hyper, by HYPER GAL - an album that is best described with highly physical metaphors: imagine you are about to be crushed and chomped alive by a big, cartoonish, spike-wielding machine (the punishingly loud cymbals and keyboards on this album), when you are briskly saved by a glimmering, dreamlike pop star (the vocals, especially in the choruses of this album). That is basically the experience of listening to “Our Hyper,” except when the machine lulls you into an industrial trance, you realize you were in good hands all along!
  5. Distracted, by Thundercat - a record with absolutely everything a good Thundercat album should have: spacey production and vocals, insanely agile and virtuosic grooves, and hilarious lyrics. It just so happens that this album also has one of the best and most well-utilized feature lists I’ve seen in a minute - ESPECIALLY “She Knows Too Much,” which features the late Mac Miller. Endlessly fun, and as smooth on the years as you would expect from Thundercat!
  6. Confession, by Carla dal Forno - a minimalist album that strips 80’s alternative styles like post punk and dream pop to its barest elements. Its minimalism creates a backdrop for various details - like bells and accordions - that stand out even more than they would normally. The vocals are unassuming but sweet, and breathe life and brightness into the mix, but the obsessive lyrics on the album give things an eerie, interesting edge, as well as the feeling of an unreliable narrator. Essential if you enjoy bands like “Au Revoir Simone.”
  7. Wendy Eisenberg, self titled - truly a gem in the oversaturated country scene! On this record, Wendy toys with traditional country and folk songwriting, through perfectly-crafted string arrangements and a playful, but sharp vocal and songwriting presence. “Meaning Business” and “Another Lifetime Floats Away” are particular standouts in how they approach memory.
  8. Frog For Sale, by Frog - a wistful indie-Americana delight from this prolific brother duo. There is truly something for everyone on “Frog For Sale,” from the 50’s rock-’n’-roll tinge of the endlessly replayable opener “Bad Time to Fall in Love Again,” to the 70’s jazziness of “Je Ne Sais Pas.”
  9. Carve, by Kathryn Mohr - a really interesting, spellbinding art rock release that harkens to PJ Harvey’s abrasive “Uh Huh Her.” The field recordings and sound collages that Kathryn compiles are as ghostly as the album cover, and the more conventionally-structured songs like “Cells” and “Angle of Repose” have a sludgy, muscular guitar tone that creates a sticky, claustrophobic tension with the vocals.
  10. Hell For A Basement, by Drug Bug - a record full of lush, proggy emo tracks that work incredibly well. It may be shamelessly nostalgic for the 70’s, but the intro track in particular has a magnetic pull.
  11. Hyperphantasia, by Lone - an album full of ebullient dance tracks that pull from and make the most of many styles - from new age (“Opening a Portal”) to speed garage (“Affinity”).
  12. Forever Ends Someday, by Wesley Joseph - a polished, but creatively-produced work of self-examination from the U.K. singer/rapper.
  13. +3, by Samba Jean-Baptiste - a cloudy, introspective r&b album that has the intimacy and jumpiness of a notebook full of Samba’s own creative ideas and personal thoughts.
  14. Reflections Vol. 3: Water Poems, by Felicia Atkinson & Christina Vantzou - an eerie and spellbinding work of naturalism, with whispered spoken-word that taps into the subconscious.
  15. Singing, by Gia Margaret - an album where Gia uses her voice as the foreground of her music for the first time since 2018, over intimate, throbbing instrumentation that unfolds slowly.

Great Albums: May 2026

  1. REDSTAR WU & THE WORLDWIDE SCOURGE, by Genesis Owusu - another masterful record from an artist who has seemingly been pouring every fibre of his being into his work since he burst onto the scene in 2021. Just as before, Genesis proves his knack for combining industrial leanings with global influences, while also making some of his most overtly political music yet. “STAMPEDE” addresses bootlickers around the world, calling for the unity of the working class over a propulsive beat. Later, “THE WORLDWIDE SCOURGE” sees Genesis denounce the genocide in Gaza, showing his willingness to see these horrendous global issues for what they are. In an industry where the mainstream is constantly selling out and disappointing fans, there will always be a place for direct and truth-telling artists like Genesis Owusu.
  2. Theft World, by Lip Critic - completely a frenetic, dark mess inspired by identity theft and conspiracy after an obsessed fan stole frontman Bret Kaser’s identity and made hundreds of purchases in his name. The fan believed that Lip Critic had hidden messages and mythology in their music. So, Lip Critic drew from this insane experience to craft an album that is equally insane. By plunging headfirst into these conspiratorial themes and building on the dark, heavy electronics of their sound, Lip Critic have created one of the knottiest and gnarliest records of the year so far.
  3. status update music, by leroy - one of the best recent examples of truly audacious sample-dependent music. On “status update music,” leroy draws from viral sounds and all kinds of iconic songs (“If You Can’t Hang” by Sleeping with Sirens????) to create their own wayward hits. It’s the sort of album that goes 110% from start to finish, and the resulting 50 minutes are euphoric. While being experimental and utterly unique, it’s also indisputable pop perfection - particularly on songs like closer “Summer Fling.”
  4. Detached from the Rest of You, by Loraine James - Since I’ve been following her work, Loraine James has been one of the most glitchy and exciting producers in the London scene. “Detached from the Rest of You” sees her take her singular sound and apply it to more direct, pop-forward song structures. While still having odd touches (such as the pitched-up vocals on tracks like “A Long Distance Call”), it feels like the most immediate record she has ever put out, even as she addresses painful topics like self doubt and detachment.
  5. Poem 1, by Ana Roxanne - this is a follow-up that I have been waiting for for YEARS! After being enamoured by “Because of a Flower” in 2020, I was so excited to see that Ana Roxanne had another project cooking, and “Poem 1” does not disappoint. Where her debut was smaller-scale but haunting in ambience, “Poem 1” sees Ana herself occupy a fuller presence in the music. She leans more into her vocals and the choir-like qualities of her compositions, which makes many songs feel like prayers.
  6. EXIT 2B, by BOYG1RL & NOVAGANG - one of the most bombastic albums of May! It’s a reinvigorating combination of reggaeton, industrial hip hop, and Haitian kompa, and a testament to how exciting “online” genres can be when they’re reworked to incorporate global influences.
  7. Rumspringa, by Ear - a pretty special album with a sprawling, yet quaint sound. Pitchfork dubbed their sound as “laptop twee” which feels like an annoying but pretty accurate microlabel. Lyrically, the album is preoccupied with the complexities of adulthood, which makes it feel truly “coming of age.”
  8. Fenian, by Kneecap - a pretty electrifying follow-up to “Fine Art” that sees the band deliver blistering critiques of colonialism over production that nods to both U.K. grime and old school West Coast hip hop.
  9. Look For Your Mind!, by The Lemon Twigs - another entry in the D’Addario brothers’ generational retro run. But this one has a special bite to it for its particularly great production, and psychedelic flourishes that feel a bit Rundgren-esque.
  10. the color of rain, by aja monet - a combination of jazz, hip hop, sound collage, and the power of the spoken word! On it, aja lets her insightful humanism unfurl and addresses a myriad of topics, from everyday growth and love to the tragedies happening in Congo.
  11. Remember the Humans, by Broken Social Scene - a surprising and jammy record that rivals this band’s best work! I loved the romantic feeling of it, the symphonic choices, and I was pleasantly surprised to see/hear Hannah Georgas on here!
  12. Members… Don’t!, by Tyshawn Sorey - in a time where a lot of jazz-adjacent music is focused on dreamy atmospherics, this reimagination of Max Roach’s work feels ironically like a breath of fresh air. It is truly fiery and immediate.
  13. For Love of Grace & the Hereafter, by Iceage - as someone who has been mostly immune to this band’s charms until this point, I found this record to be incredibly catchy, energetic, and full of addictive 2000’s nostalgia. It’s the most I’ve enjoyed Iceage.
  14. Symbols, by Sergeant - very ghostly, mostly in how the band seem to shred up their sound and paste it back together to create a detached, but psychedelic sound.
  15. Same Fangs, by Spencer Krug - a spectral-feeling collection of indie rock songs that I haven’t been able to shake. A must-listen for anyone who enjoys a bit of Destroyer now and then.

Great Albums: June 2026

  1. So Help Me God, by Kelsey Lu
  2. Eyes Full, by Zoh Amba
  3. Profound, by Ruth Garbus
  4. The weathervane is my body, by Truck Violence -
  5. Spontaneous Music Live, by SML - a genuine breath of fresh air. With the music industry becoming increasingly digitized and with that, curatorial, SML have subverted album expectations by creating an album that is completely improvised and unedited. The resulting music feels like a “lightning in a bottle” moment - with sparse passages weaving into something groovy and psychedelic, and listeners being able to appreciate the contemplative space around moments that could have been edited into more traditional “songs.”
  6. L.U.V., by CFCF - strobe-lit, romantic “recession pop” that, while fun, never feels careless or thrown together. From opener “Kiss Me,” this record is basically electropop perfection from beginning to end, and it approaches the genre with both irony and affection. Edgier production choices every now and then prevent it from sounding too generic or overly nostalgic. Definitely a good listen for anyone who enjoyed Ninajirachi’s “I Love My Computer.”
  7. The Ground Above, by Beth Orton - utterly meditative. “The Ground Above,” for me, taps into a similar vein that Beth Gibbons’ album “Lives Outgrown” did in 2024. Beth Orton’s voice is arresting in how it tremors through phrases, and she is supported by gorgeous, jazzy arrangements that call back to late-80s Talk Talk. The tracks on this album often take their time to unfold, but gradually build into spellbinding moments. In the meantime, she confronts grief, mortality, and life in general, sounding equally mournful and persevering.
  8. Ultra, by Six Sex - a club-ready Argentinian album teeming with fun and absurdity. Definitely one of the more enjoyable results of the recent electroclash craze for me, with its tracks dealing in cartoonish violence and subverted hypersexuality. It might be pretty on-the-nose, but I really enjoy what it does - especially with Six Sex’s playful, confident delivery.
  9. NATURE IS HEALING, by horsegiirL - possibly the most highly anticipated album of 2026 for me! I have been enamoured with horsegiirL ever since I discovered her through her utterly insane single “eat, sleep, slay, repeat” in 2024. While the lead single “an apple a day” showed the German musician in a similar lane, “NATURE IS HEALING” is a vaguely conceptual album with an omnivorous sound (from 80s new wave to 2010s EDM, and plenty of Y2K pop vibes in between) that solidifies horsegiirL as more than a novelty artist.
  10. in this body, by sparklmami - short, sweet, and irresistible. In only 9 songs and 24 minutes, sparklmami manages to tap into an infectious blend of disco, funk, bedroom pop, and Brazillian jazz to create an album that feels like her own little world. Lush opener “no te vayas” practically oozes with retro pastiche, and its confidently-delivered, warm spoken word passages make it an incredible tone setter. The vibe only becomes more weird and wonderful with the jazziness of tracks like “fajas.”
  11. Guest List, by M. Geddes Gengras - undoubtedly his least ambient album yet, “Guest List” sees M. Geddes Gengras collaborate with a sleuth of musicians to create an ambitious, ever-shifting electronic record that dazzles with post-rock intention.
  12. You Seem Pretty Sad for a Girl So in Love, by Olivia Rodrigo - all-consuming romance followed by heartbreak might be a tale as old as time for a pop record, but Olivia makes this trope her own on her nostalgic, tastefully-arranged 3rd album.
  13. Demand to be Taken to Heaven Alive!, by Horse Lords - a record that finds the virtuosic band taking their brainy, kraut-y sound to more spiritual places.
  14. Doctrine of Love, by Jalen Ngonda - a near-perfect recreation of 60’s Motown with big arrangements, big melodies, and big feelings.
  15. Somewhere Good, by Tara Clerkin Trio - a warm, sunny record that refreshingly updates a classic trip hop sound.
  16. Critical Thot, by DJ Haram & Sha Ray - dark and uncompromising, “Critical Thot” is an impressive debut for Sha Ray, and another glimpse of DJ Haram’s dizzying production chops.
  17. Lucid, by Micah Thomas - a pensive record that sees the piano talent lean into more avant-garde structures and chord progressions to produce an overarching album that feels dreamlike.
  18. Harmony, by Brutalismus 3000 - a record that finds the Berlin duo anarchistic at every turn.
  19. Spatial, No Problem, by Lee “Scratch” Perry & Mouse on Mars - the late Jamaican legend’s final record is so imaginative and joyous, that calling it a “posthumous album” almost feels reductive.
  20. Your Day Will Come, by Chanel Beads - eclectic pop music that feels both infectious and dizzying through its spacy production.
